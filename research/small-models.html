Small local models on the 8 GB Mac mini

Comparison of the installed Ollama models for high-risk, low-cognitive enterprise tasks — cost, security, local data, latency. Generated 7 Sep 2026. Machine-readable data: small-models.json

The seven unique chat models installed on this machine divide into three honest bands: a 1.7–3 B band that runs comfortably, a 4 B model whose usefulness collapses with context length, and Google’s Gemma 4 E4B — an effective-4B design that arrives as a 9.6 GB Q4 bundle (8 B total once embeddings and the vision/audio encoders count) and runs at roughly 13 tok/s steady state. The single most important number below is still the qwen3:4b context cliff — roughly 1 tok/s at 32 K context against 19.5 tok/s at 8 K. For local data with latency as a requirement, context budgeting decides which models are usable at all, and file size decides how often a cold start costs you half a minute.

Small models are not cheaper versions of large ones; they are a different tool. IBM's Granite line, Microsoft's Phi line and Meta's Llama 3.2 smalls are all positioned the same way: the 1–4 B tier exists for latency- and privacy-bound tasks where the decision is narrow and the format is strict, while judgement tasks stay on bigger models reached through a router.

Pattern: edge silicon vendors modify (quantise, export to NPU runtimes); software products mostly deploy unmodified or lightly quantised weights; teams that need a specific output contract fine-tune. Nobody productises a 1–4 B model without a guardrail or grounding layer around it.

Tuning for high-risk, low-cognitive tasks

The target slice — narrow decisions with strict formats where a wrong answer is costly — fails small models in three distinct ways, and each has a different fix:

Failure modeFixWhy
Wrong specific facts (thresholds, policy numbers, schema fields) Grounding — knowledge bundles in Google Open Knowledge Format (OKF), then RAG for the long tail Facts belong in retrievable, versioned documents, not in weights. OKF is a directory of Markdown files, one concept per file, with YAML frontmatter (type, sources, verified, stale_after) and links between concepts as the graph; the agent reads only the files it needs. That is small-model-sized context by design
Unstable format and behaviour (JSON drift, wrong tone, missing fields) Fine-tuning (LoRA / QLoRA) plus constrained decoding and schema enforcement Fine-tune for the output contract and domain register, not for facts. 500–5,000 examples of the exact task shape, human-reviewed; then quantise to GGUF and import into Ollama
Not enough reasoning depth Do not fine-tune. Route up to L2–L4 through the gateway Small models cannot be trained into judgement; escalate and attribute the cost

OKF in practice on this machine

  • One bundle per domain: policy and threshold lookups, schema definitions, runbooks, decision tables — the stable facts a decision must cite.
  • Add the trust fields from the OKF v0.2 layer (verified: human:..., stale_after) and treat them as audit hooks; for high-risk classes a human review of the bundle is the control, and stale_after dates drive re-review.
  • Keep bundles small: with the Mac mini's context economics, a bundle that fits in 4–8 K tokens keeps qwen3:1.7b at ~40 tok/s and llama3.2:3b at ~25 tok/s. Long RAG dumps push prompt-processing time up and token/s down.
  • Use nomic-embed-text (already installed) to index bundle files and route: known-knowns read straight from the bundle graph; the long tail goes to RAG over the same embeddings; both feed a small re-ranker or simply the model's cited-context window.
  • The OKF spec is young (announced June 2026) and evolving — check the current spec under GoogleCloudPlatform/knowledge-catalog before committing tooling.

Fine-tuning in practice

  • Fine-tune only after the grounding design exists, and keep facts out of the weights: facts change, weights do not, and re-training per policy update is the expensive path.
  • On this 8 GB machine, LoRA / QLoRA on the 1.7–3 B models is feasible locally (Unsloth or Llama-Factory); for a serious enterprise run, train on a cloud free tier and bring the quantised GGUF back.
  • License check before fine-tuning for a product: Qwen2.5-3B and Qwen2.5-Coder-3B carry the Qwen Research License; Qwen3 models are Apache 2.0, Llama 3.2 has a >700M-MAU clause, DeepSeek-R1 is MIT.
  • Regression-test every fine-tune and every quantisation change against a golden task set before rollout; this is where a local eval harness earns its keep.

Gemma 4 E4B in the same frame

Gemma joins the fleet table above; against the trio it is a different generation (Apr 2026 vs 2024–2025) and resets several of the older trade-offs:

  • Size semantics changed: “E4B” = 4.5B effective parameters of an 8.0B-total dense model — per-layer embeddings plus ~450M of vision/audio encoders inflate the file, not the active brain. Google is explicit: “the E in E2B and E4B stands for effective parameters.” It is not MoE.
  • Built, not pruned or distilled: the card claims a from-scratch family built from “the same world-class research and technology as Gemini 3”; a configurable thinking mode, native function calling, JSON output and system-role support ship in the base instruct weights.
  • Multimodal where the trio is text-only: image and audio input (140+ languages) make it the only installed model that could read a scanned form or a voice note without a pipeline.
  • License reset: Apache 2.0 — Google’s first Apache Gemma family, a deliberate move away from the old Gemma license, framed as “developer flexibility and digital sovereignty.”
  • On paper it outclasses the trio (vendor table): MMLU-Pro 69.4, GPQA-D 58.6, AIME-2026 42.5, LiveCodeBench v6 52.0, MMMLU 76.6. None of the three rivals publishes numbers in that range at their size.
  • On this machine it is the slowest of the four: ~13 tok/s warm, 8.0 tok/s on a ~39 s cold first load, because the 9.6 GB Q4 bundle exceeds comfortable resident RAM. The fix is Google’s own QAT / mobile builds (E4B ~3.65 GB under LiteRT-LM; E2B mobile format ~1 GB) — that is the build worth benchmarking next, not the bundle installed today.
  • Real-user signal is young but real: HN commenters expect E2B/E4B to “move the pareto frontier” once ranked; tool calling is the early complaint; and users report Gemma 4 is dramatically less refusal-prone than Gemma 3 out of the box.

What this decides for a high-risk, low-cognitive workload

  • The latency-critical L1 default stays qwen3:1.7b on this machine — nearly 2× the generation speed of its nearest peer at a fraction of the RAM.
  • Reach for llama3.2:3b when the output contract is format-heavy or the context is long; it is the only 2024-vintage model here with a real vendor tooling and ecosystem story.
  • deepseek-r1:1.5b is a reasoning component, not an answer model: at the 160-token fixture budget it produced zero visible output. If you keep it, budget 512+ tokens for thinking and parse the final block.
  • gemma4:e4b deserves a re-test with the QAT build (roughly a third of the file size) before you judge it; at 9.6 GB it is the wrong artifact for an 8 GB daily driver, and its benchmark profile says the capability is worth the second look.

How to read any of this without being misled: (1) vendor-reported numbers come from each maker’s own harness and few-shot setup — only same-harness runs (Artificial Analysis, your own) are directly comparable; (2) check whether a score is for the base or the instruct checkpoint; (3) reasoning-mode pass@1 and direct answers are different tests; (4) benchmarks drift across years (MATH vs MATH-500, AIME 2024 vs 2026), so compare like years only; (5) for high-risk work, the leaderboard is a filter, not a verdict — build a golden set of 8–12 of your own fixtures (real inputs, schema-checked outputs), score format compliance and field accuracy as well as answer correctness, and re-run it after every quantisation or fine-tune. That set is the only benchmark that predicts your deployment.

What is missing or worth focusing on

  1. Task-level evaluation does not exist yet. The fleet has been measured for speed, not for capability on the actual tasks. Build a golden set of 8–12 fixtures from the real task classes and score the models before tuning anything. Until then, model choice is guesswork.
  2. The 8 GB ceiling shapes the whole claim. This machine validates the L1 edge tier only. qwen3:4b at 32 K context is unusable here; a 4–8 B enterprise tier needs real hardware. Position the analysis accordingly and keep context budgets short.
  3. High-risk is a format and audit problem before it is an accuracy problem. Spend on constrained decoding, schema validation, guardrails, human checkpoints for the risky classes, and versioned knowledge — not on bigger models. Your GenAI Gateway (router, guardrails, eval, audit, cost dashboard) is the natural home for exactly this layer.
  4. Grounding vs fine-tuning is the wrong split. The productive split is: facts to OKF bundles and RAG, behaviour and format to fine-tuning, reasoning depth to routing up. Doing only one of these is the most common miss.
  5. Latency math includes retrieval and prompt processing. A small model with a bloated context loses its advantage. Measure end-to-end: retrieval + prompt + generation, per task, not just generation token/s.
  6. Licensing and drift are operational risks, not research questions. The two Qwen2.5-3B models are research-licensed; Llama 3.2 has an MAU clause. Track drift and staleness of the knowledge bundles as production controls.
