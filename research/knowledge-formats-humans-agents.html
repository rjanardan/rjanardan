1 What is knowledge

Knowledge sits above data and information, and below wisdom. The useful definition for this guide is practical: knowledge is structured understanding that lets a person or a system act correctly.

The classical ladder, popularised by the management theorist Russell Ackoff in 1989, treats the terms as a stack:[Ackoff 1989]

LayerDefinitionExample in an enterprise setting
DataRaw recorded signals with no interpretation“12,408 requests, 14:02 UTC”
InformationData arranged to answer who, what, when, where“Requests from Bengaluru peaked at 14:02”
KnowledgeInformation plus interpretation: how things work, what to do“Peak-hour bursts are caused by the 14:00 report job; scale the read pool before 14:00”
Understanding / wisdomJudgement about why and whether; knowing when rules do not apply“The report job matters less than the new compliance query; change the schedule instead”

Philosophy adds a caution that matters for AI systems. The standard definition of knowledge as justified true belief collapses under cases where a belief happens to be true but is not justified (the Gettier problem, 1963). An agent that produces a correct answer for a wrong reason has a belief, not knowledge. This distinction is not academic: it is the difference between a model that answers correctly because it was given a verified source, and one that answers correctly by luck. The whole architecture of verification discussed in this guide exists to move systems from the second kind to the first.

Michael Polanyi’s distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge is the second foundation stone. “We can know more than we can tell,” Polanyi wrote in 1966: riding a bicycle, judging a weld, or estimating a schedule are things people know how to do without being able to write the procedure down.[Polanyi 1966] Organisational theorists Nonaka and Takeuchi built a whole school on converting tacit knowledge to explicit form and back, through socialisation, externalisation, combination and internalisation (the SECI cycle).[Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995] Every “knowledge management” failure of the 1990s and 2000s was, at root, a failure of one of the two conversions: experts would not or could not externalise what they knew, or explicit documents were never internalised by the people who needed them.

Knowledge representation research contributes a third distinction: knowledge as something that can be encoded. In that tradition knowledge is a set of statements about a domain — classes, individuals, relations, rules — written in a language with a defined syntax and semantics, so that a machine can store it, query it and reason over it.[Brachman & Levesque 2004] This guide uses a working definition that sits at the intersection of all three traditions:

Working definition. Knowledge is justified, structured understanding of a domain — partly tacit, partly explicit — that enables correct action. Explicit knowledge becomes machine-usable only when it is encoded in a format whose conventions are shared between producer and consumer. The format is the contract; the knowledge is the content; the agent is a new kind of consumer.

Four properties of knowledge recur throughout this guide:

  • It is non-rivalrous. One person’s use does not consume it; this drives its strange economics (section 11).
  • It degrades. Facts go stale, procedures change, documents rot. Without a life cycle (section 9) it silently becomes misinformation.
  • It is relational. A fact is only useful in a web of other facts — thresholds with their owning team, policies with their effective dates, concepts with their definitions.
  • It is now read by machines as first-class consumers. This is the change of the past three years, and it is why the format of knowledge, not just its content, has become a strategic question.

2 Knowledge in the agentic-AI era

Agents have changed where knowledge lives: less of it can stay in model weights, and more of it must arrive in context at inference time, in a shape the agent can navigate.

Three limits of parametric knowledge drive the shift. First, staleness: a model knows only what was in its training corpus up to its cutoff. The small models used in section 8 were trained before mid-2026; Google’s Open Knowledge Format was announced in June 2026, so no pretrained model of that vintage can answer a question about it from weights alone — they must be told. Second, hallucination: language models generate plausible continuations, and when a fact is absent or thinly represented they confabulate with the same fluency as when it is present. Third, mutable knowledge: thresholds, prices, policies and schemas change on a cadence no retraining cycle can match. The industry answer, arrived at independently by many teams, is to keep volatile knowledge in documents and retrieval systems, and keep the model as a reasoning engine over whatever context it is given. Retrieval-augmented generation, introduced in 2020 and surveyed by Gao et al., formalised the pattern of fetch-then-answer;[Gao et al. 2023, arXiv 2312.10997] graph-based variants such as Microsoft’s GraphRAG added global, query-independent structure on top of local chunks.[Edge et al. 2024, arXiv 2404.16130]

The agentic turn goes further. An agent does not answer one question; it pursues a goal across many turns, calls tools, and decides what to read. Its working memory is the context window, and the discipline of deciding what goes into that window has acquired a name: context engineering. Anthropic’s engineering team published the reference essay on the practice, and a research line has formed around agentic context engineering — models that decide for themselves which context to gather and retain.[Anthropic engineering 2025][Zhang et al. 2025, arXiv 2510.04618] Andrej Karpathy described the end-state most vividly in his mid-2025 “LLM wiki” writings, quoted in Google’s announcement: “LLMs don’t get bored, don’t forget to update a cross-reference, and can touch 15 files in one pass.” The bookkeeping that makes human-maintained wikis decay is exactly what agents are good at — if the knowledge is in files an agent can read.[Google Cloud 2026a]

Three consequences follow for knowledge management:

  • Knowledge must be addressable. An agent cannot use knowledge it cannot find; it needs file paths, filenames, titles, descriptions, tags — handles a retrieval system can surface and a model can reason over. The Tenure benchmark of OKF (section 8.3) showed that giving a model readable filenames and frontmatter measurably beat opaque vector recall on alias-style queries (“Mongo” → the MongoDB decision).[Tenure 2026]
  • Knowledge must be trustworthy before it is read. An agent that acts on a stale policy is worse than an agent that asks. Provenance, verification and expiry metadata — the “trust signals” layer of OKF v0.2 — let a consumer decide whether to even open a file (section 7.2).
  • Knowledge needs a standard shape. Tool interfaces standardised first — the Model Context Protocol (MCP), released by Anthropic in November 2024, became a founding project of the Linux Foundation’s Agentic AI Foundation in December 2025; Google’s agent-to-agent protocol A2A (April 2025) followed into the same foundation in August 2026; OpenAI’s AGENTS.md convention for agent instructions was a third founding donation.[AAIF 2025][AAIF 2026] But the layer where agents keep truth — memory, persistent knowledge, the record of what happened — still has no standard. Commentators on the A2A transfer made the point explicitly: “memory still has no standard.”[OpenZync 2026] OKF is Google Cloud’s move into exactly that gap.

The competitive context sharpens the point. Enterprise work-knowledge platforms (Glean, Hebbia, Contextual AI), model vendors’ memory products, and a layer of startups building “agent memory” infrastructure (Mem0, Letta, Zep, Cognee — surveyed in section D) are all racing to be the place where an organisation’s knowledge lives for its agents. Whoever owns the format of that knowledge owns a durable migration cost on their side. Open formats are the counter-move: they make the knowledge portable even if the tool is not. That is the strategic bet of OKF, and it is why a guide about “formats” rather than about models is the right frame in 2026.

Section 8 tests the core claim on small local models: does attaching a knowledge bundle change what a 1.7–4 B model can answer, and at what cost in latency and honesty?

3 How knowledge helps humans in communication

Communication works because participants share knowledge they never state. The less shared knowledge exists, the more must be made explicit — and formats are the technology for making it explicit at scale.

In the pragmatics tradition, every utterance rides on common ground: the beliefs both parties know they share.[Clark 1996] “Ship it” works in a team that knows what “it” is, what shipping means in this organisation, and what the risks are. Grice’s maxims — say no more than needed, be relevant — are only affordable when the unstated substrate is large.[Grice 1975] Jargon is compression: a specialist term (a class name, a clinical code, a legal doctrine) folds a large shared understanding into one token. This is why vocabulary and taxonomy work (section 4) are not pedantry; they are bandwidth technology for groups.

Human groups scale their shared knowledge through externalisation — writing knowledge down so it survives the people who hold it. The sequence is familiar: oral tradition bound knowledge to memory and presence; writing freed it from both; print made it cheap; libraries made it findable; the web made it universal; wikis made it editable; git made it versioned. Each step raised how much knowledge a group could assume rather than re-derive. Nonaka and Takeuchi’s SECI cycle describes the organisational version of the same movement: tacit insight, externalised into explicit documents, combined with other documents, and internalised by new people as skill.[Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995]

In modern engineering organisations the coordination artifacts are well known: runbooks, architecture decision records, onboarding guides, incident postmortems, API documentation, READMEs. Anthropologist Susan Leigh Star called such shared artifacts boundary objects — things plastic enough to serve different communities’ purposes while stable enough to carry meaning across them.[Star & Griesemer 1989] A runbook is a boundary object between an on-call engineer and the service owner; a clinical guideline is one between a physician and a hospital’s quality team. The important shift of the 2020s is that the boundary object now has a third party reading it: the agent. The same document must remain legible to a human expert and be navigable, parseable and trustworthy to a machine that will act on it.

Two human communication problems sharpen when agents join the conversation:

  • The tacit floor. Polanyi’s point cuts both ways: teams that rely on hallway knowledge cannot hand it to agents, because it was never written down. “Ask the team” does not work for a model. Documented knowledge stops being optional the moment automation is expected to act on it — this is the least glamorous and most common reason agent projects stall.
  • Asynchrony without loss. Text-based communities — open-source projects, forums, wikis — coordinate across time zones precisely because their knowledge is written in a shared, retrievable form.[Eghbal 2020] Agents inherit this property only if the artefacts they read carry the same discipline: one concept per file, stable identifiers, links between related knowledge, and metadata about freshness and trust. Those are format properties, not model properties.

Section 7’s subject — a directory of markdown files, one concept per file, YAML frontmatter, links between files — should be read as the current best attempt to give boundary objects a shape that both humans and agents can inhabit comfortably. It is deliberately close to what human teams already write; that is its design bet.

4 The vocabulary of knowledge

Taxonomies, ontologies and knowledge graphs are increasingly formal ways of saying what exists and how it relates. They form the vocabulary ladder that the newer, lighter formats climb.

Controlled vocabularies and taxonomies

A controlled vocabulary is an agreed list of terms (product codes, statuses, region names) that removes the cost of synonyms. A taxonomy arranges terms in a hierarchy of parent–child relations: Linnaean biology, the Dewey Decimal Classification, an organisation chart, a folder tree. Taxonomies give a group shared nouns and a shared notion of generality. Their limits are that one term can belong in several places (a budget report is finance and programme management), which pure trees cannot express. Folksonomies — free-form tags — solve flexibility by abandoning control, at the price of synonymy (“ml”, “ML”, “machine-learning”) and inconsistency.

Ontologies

An ontology is a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualisation: the classes, properties, individuals and axioms of a domain, written in a language with defined semantics.[Gruber 1993] Where a taxonomy says “A is a kind of B”, an ontology adds arbitrary relations, cardinalities and logical constraints, so that a reasoner can derive facts that were never stated. The formal backbone is description logic; the practical encodings are the W3C family — RDF, RDFS, OWL, SKOS, SHACL — plus JSON-LD for embedding in web pages.[W3C stack; Allemang & Hendler 2020] Mature examples include schema.org (the shared type system of the web’s structured data), SNOMED CT and ICD in healthcare, the Gene Ontology in biology, and Dublin Core for documents. The price of formality is labour: ontologies are expensive to build and to keep aligned with the world, which is why most organisations never got past a taxonomy or a graph sketch.

Knowledge graphs

A knowledge graph is an ontology populated with data: nodes for entities, typed edges for relations, and a query layer over both.[Hogan et al. 2021] Google’s 2012 knowledge graph popularised the term for search; Wikidata (2012, now well over 120 million items) is the largest public one; enterprise graphs run on both RDF stores (Ontotext, Stardog) and property-graph databases (Neo4j, with the GQL standard arriving in 2024). Knowledge graphs shine at multi-hop questions, disambiguation and consistency; they cost more than documents to maintain.

Where the new file-based formats sit

The 2025–26 generation of formats — AGENTS.md, llms.txt, OKF — deliberately descend the formality ladder. They trade logical rigour for the properties agents actually need: plain-text addressability, cheap human maintenance, git-native versioning. OKF’s graph is literally the links between markdown files; its “ontology” is an optional type field with suggested values, not enforced classes (section 7.1). The ladder is not a competition — a team can hold a formal ontology for regulatory reporting and an OKF bundle for operational runbooks, and link the two. The useful mental model:

The Tenure benchmark result that aliases resolve well (“Mongo” → the MongoDB document) is exactly the controlled-vocabulary benefit reappearing inside a file system: names, titles and tags give the model handles that raw embeddings lack.[Tenure 2026]

5 Representation standards and non-standards

Knowledge representation has a forty-year standardisation history, a mature web-era stack, and a fast-moving set of 2025–26 conventions that are standards in all but name.

The legacy of knowledge representation

AI’s first generation tried to encode knowledge directly: semantic networks (Quillian, 1960s), frames (Minsky, 1974), scripts (Schank & Abelson, 1977), and the decade-long Cyc attempt to codify common sense (Lenat, from 1984).[Brachman & Levesque 2004] These systems produced the vocabulary — classes, slots, inheritance, defaults — that later standardisation formalised, and taught the field its durable lesson: encoding is the bottleneck, and maintenance is the killer. The formal-logic tradition continued separately in description logics and in the KR&R textbook canon.

There is a clear sequencing logic in this table. The industry standardised how agents act (MCP for tool access, A2A for agent-to-agent), then how agents are instructed (AGENTS.md), and is now moving to what agents should know (OKF) and how sites describe themselves to LLMs (llms.txt). The foundation transfers — MCP, goose, AGENTS.md into AAIF in December 2025, A2A in August 2026 — are the moment each convention stopped belonging to one vendor. Google Cloud chose a different route for OKF: a spec in a public repository (GoogleCloudPlatform/knowledge-catalog, okf/ directory) with a permissive license, community contributions and sample bundles, without (yet) a foundation transfer.

The non-standards that dominate practice

Most organisational knowledge today lives in formats with no agreed contract at all: bespoke wikis (“each instance bespoke”, in the words of the OKF announcement), chat transcripts treated as memory, exported PDFs, prompt files stuffed with context, private vector stores with no interchange format, and vendor-specific memory APIs whose contents cannot leave the product. The v0.1 announcement names the failure mode precisely: markdown wikis “may all look alike, but none of them are intentionally designed to cooperate. There is no agreed-upon answer to what fields every document should carry, or what filenames mean what.”[Google Cloud 2026a] Non-standards are not wrong — they are where conventions are born — but they are where knowledge goes to die when the tool that holds it is retired.

6 Why standardisation is required

Standardisation is a coordination technology: it lets independent producers and consumers cooperate without negotiating. In the agentic era the producers and consumers are often not even in the same organisation, and one of them is software.

Six arguments, in increasing order of force:

  • Portability and exit rights. Knowledge stored in a documented format can move between tools, vendors and agents. Knowledge stored in a proprietary memory blob cannot. For enterprises this is the difference between owning their knowledge and renting it (the economics are developed in section 11).
  • Producer/consumer independence. The OKF spec’s own principle: a team that publishes knowledge does not need to know which agent will read it, and an agent builder does not need to know which team wrote it. A fixed minimum contract (type; later, optional trust fields) is all either side needs.[OKF SPEC v0.2]
  • Agent economics. An agent facing N incompatible knowledge shapes must write N parsers or, worse, guess. A standard shape makes retrieval cheaper, makes progressive disclosure possible (read the index, then only the files you need), and lets a consumer apply policy before reading a file — OKF v0.2’s trust fields exist so that “the consumer can decide whether to even open the document”.[Google Cloud 2026b]
  • Governance and audit. Versioning, expiry, ownership and provenance are only enforceable if the fields that carry them are predictable. The knowledge life cycle of section 9 — creation, review, deprecation, erasure — needs a substrate where those states are machine-readable. The OKF community’s “erasure conformance” contribution is a direct answer to privacy law: a profile that lets a bundle prove a deletion happened.
  • Network effects of shared vocabulary. Every tool that reads the same shape multiplies the value of every document written in it (section 4’s vocabulary ladder, applied to machines). This is why the foundation transfers of MCP, AGENTS.md and A2A happened: competitors donate plumbing to a neutral steward rather than fragment the ecosystem — “competitors do not hand their protocol plumbing to a mutual steward unless the alternative, every vendor running its own incompatible stack, is worse.”[OpenZync 2026]
  • Safety. Guardrails need inspectable knowledge. A policy that an agent must obey is only auditable if it exists as a document with a defined shape, a verification record and an expiry date — not if it is a pattern the model picked up from training data. Standardised, verified knowledge is the difference between “the agent should know this” and “we can prove the agent was told this, and that the telling was current” (section 10).

The honest costs

Standardisation also has real costs, and the designers of OKF priced them explicitly. A heavy ontology imposes a vocabulary tax that kills adoption; OKF’s answer is “minimally opinionated” — one required field, suggested rather than enforced types, and optional trust fields where “absence carries meaning”.[OKF SPEC v0.2] Premature standardisation locks in today’s mistakes; the remedy is versioned, additive evolution (v0.1 → v0.2 kept every v0.1 document valid, renaming timestamp to generated.at while preserving a fallback). And standards can become moats rather than commons — which is why governance neutrality (AAIF) and open repositories (knowledge-catalog) matter more than the spec text itself. The test of a healthy standard is whether the originator can no longer unilaterally change its direction, and whether the knowledge survives the tool.

7 Deep dive: Google’s Open Knowledge Format

The Open Knowledge Format (OKF) is an open specification, first published by Google Cloud in June 2026, for portable knowledge bundles that humans and agents can share. v0.1 fixed the shape; v0.2 added the trust layer. Both versions are defined in one normative document, okf/SPEC.md in the public GoogleCloudPlatform/knowledge-catalog repository, with reference tooling and four sample bundles (acme_retail, ga4, stackoverflow, crypto_bitcoin).[OKF SPEC v0.2]

7.1  v0.1: the LLM-wiki pattern, made portable

OKF v0.1, announced 13 June 2026 by Sam McVeety and Amir Hormati of Google Cloud, is an answer to a fragmentation problem. Since 2025, teams had converged independently on the same shape for machine-readable knowledge: markdown files, one concept per file, YAML frontmatter, hyperlinks between files. Karpathy’s “LLM wiki” made the idea famous — “LLMs don’t get bored, don’t forget to update a cross-reference, and can touch 15 files in one pass” — and Obsidian vaults, AGENTS.md conventions and internal wikis spread the pattern. But, as the announcement put it, the instances were bespoke: “There is no agreed-upon answer to what fields every document should carry, or what filenames mean what.” OKF v0.1 formalised the small set of conventions needed to make the pattern interoperable.[Google Cloud 2026a]

The v0.1 contract is deliberately tiny. A bundle is a directory tree of markdown files; each file is one concept, and links between files are the graph. Two filenames are reserved and optional: index.md, a directory listing that enables progressive disclosure, and log.md, a chronological, date-grouped change log.[OKF SPEC v0.2 §3, §8, §9] Frontmatter carries the metadata:

---
type: BigQuery Table            # REQUIRED — the only required field
title: Customer Orders          # recommended, human-readable
description: One row per completed customer order across all channels.
resource: https://console.cloud.google.com/bigquery?p=acme&d=sales&t=orders
tags: [sales, orders, revenue]
---

type is free-form and unregistered: the spec’s examples include BigQuery Table, Metric, Playbook, API Endpoint, Reference and Attested Computation, and consumers MUST tolerate unknown types by treating them as generic concepts. A concept carrying only type is fully conformant. Bodies are structural markdown with a few conventional headings (# Schema, # Examples, # Computation) but no required sections. Three principles anchor the design: minimally opinionated (one required field, so adoption is cheap), producer/consumer independence (a writer need not know the reader), and format not platform (no runtime, no vendor, no lock-in).[OKF SPEC v0.2 §4]

The launch shipped reference tooling that made the format real rather than aspirational: a reference enrichment agent that turns BigQuery assets into concept markdown with citations, a static HTML visualiser for browsing bundles, and ingestion into Google’s Knowledge Catalog. The tools were explicitly secondary: “The format itself is the contribution.”[Google Cloud 2026a] The accompanying sample bundles showed the intended reading experience: an agent (or human) opens index.md, sees the directory of concepts grouped by section, and reads only the files its task requires.

7.2  v0.2: trust signals

v0.2, published 25 July 2026 by the same authors, answers a question v0.1 left open: how does a consumer know whether a concept is worth reading? The design goal is stated in the announcement: make provenance, trust, freshness, lifecycle state and computational attestation answerable “from frontmatter alone, before the consumer reads the body” — trust filtering before any token is spent. All the new families are optional; the spec keeps the vocabulary, not the rules, and “absence carries meaning.”[Google Cloud 2026b] The normative details:[OKF SPEC v0.2 §5, §10]

  • Provenance — sources, not a score. A concept lists the materials it derives from: resource (required within an entry), optional id for per-claim attribution, title, and three objective credibility signals — author (an actor, e.g. team:ga4-docs), usage_count over a usage_window (a liveness signal, explicitly not a ranking score), and last_modified. The spec’s refusal to store a credibility score is deliberate: a score is subjective, unportable across consumers, and goes stale. Attribution inside the body uses markdown footnotes keyed to sources[].id — keyed rather than positional, because agents rewrite these documents and a positional index silently misattributes as soon as the list is reordered.
  • Trust — generated and verified. generated: {by, at} records who or what wrote the current content; verified lists independent confirmation events, each {by, at}. Writers and confirmers are kept distinct on purpose: who authored a concept need not be who confirmed it. Consumers derive a three-tier trust ladder from verified: no key → unverified; non-human verifiers only → machine-confirmed; any human:<id> verifier → human-reviewed. The actor convention reserves the human: prefix for hand-authored or human-confirmed content, and tiers are advisory signals, never access control.
  • Freshness — stale_after. An absolute ISO 8601 instant, compared as now >= stale_after. An absolute instant rather than a relative TTL keeps staleness a plain comparison “with no reference to when the concept was read” — deterministic for agents that may be offline or replaying.
  • Lifecycle — status. draft | stable | deprecated; absent means stable. Deprecated concepts stay in the tree for links and history but are no longer current.
  • Attested computations. A new concept type, type: Attested Computation, carries “not just what a value means but a sanctioned way to compute it.” Its contract: a runtime (required; bigquery, postgres, dbt, python …), typed parameters (the only thing an agent may fill), the computation itself (inline under # Computation or in a file), an executor with a declared receipt (e.g. BigQuery job_id plus the SQL that actually ran), and an attester — deterministic, no-LLM code that re-derives the binding and compares it against the receipt. A rewritten query, a swapped computation file or a mutated dependency fails the check. The consumer flow is: discover, load, parameterise, execute, attest, gate — refusing to display a failing attestation and warning when stale_after has passed. Verification and attestation are distinct and both exist: verified confirms the definition still matches policy (doc-level, slow, stored in the bundle); attestation confirms a single run produced the value the sanctioned way (per-call, runtime, not stored). OKF records the computation and the means to check it; it never executes anything itself.

v0.2 is an additive minor bump with two deliberate exceptions: timestamp was renamed to generated.at, and the v0.1 body # Citations list moved into frontmatter as sources; in both cases consumers MAY fall back to the v0.1 form, so every v0.1 bundle remains consumable.[OKF SPEC v0.2 §13] Community contributions listed in the announcement show the format is already evolving outside Google: typed relationship edges, agent-routing hints, an erasure conformance profile (deletion with proof, for privacy law), a .okfignore, more sample bundles, and a growing ecosystem catalog of third-party tools — Claude Code skills, builders and converters (see section D).

7.3  What OKF is, and is not

Positioning OKF honestly requires saying what it is not, because the failure modes are informative.

OKF is an interchange and discovery format, not a retrieval system. The Tenure benchmark (section 8.3) put an OKF bundle inside a real AI tool and let the model choose which files to read: single-turn precision was 0.47, alias-style queries were strong (0.72 precision / 0.92 recall), but scope disambiguation, supersession and session continuity failed, and session-mode pass rate collapsed to 0.17. Its conclusion — “OKF solves interchange. It does not solve selection” — is the single most useful one-paragraph critique of the format published so far.[Tenure 2026]

OKF is not an ontology, though it is graph-shaped. Its type vocabulary is unregistered and its edges are plain markdown links. There is no reasoner, no SHACL validation, no query language. Teams that need formal semantics still need the W3C stack; OKF’s wager is that most agent knowledge does not. The SPEC’s permissive conformance clause is explicit: consumers MUST NOT reject a bundle for missing optional fields, unknown types, unknown keys, broken links, or a missing index.md.[OKF SPEC v0.2 §11]

OKF is a sibling of AGENTS.md, llms.txt and MCP, not a competitor. AGENTS.md instructs a coding agent about a repository; llms.txt tells crawlers what a website contains; MCP is the transport an agent uses to reach tools and data (an OKF bundle can be served over MCP — the AWS benchmark run in section 8.3 consumed its bundle that way); OKF is the content layer: durable, curated knowledge with trust metadata. The layer above OKF — which files to retrieve, what to exclude, what is current for this turn, this user, this task — is still open, and is exactly where the memory-layer startups in section D are competing.[OpenZync 2026]

What is genuinely new in OKF is the trust vocabulary. Markdown wikis existed; the field names, the human:/machine-confirmed trust ladder, the absolute-expiry rule, and the attester contract are the contribution. They convert “trust this document” from a vibe into a machine-readable decision, which is what an autonomous consumer needs. Whether the vocabulary is the right one will be decided by the ecosystem, not by the spec — and the fact that a spec this young already has third-party benchmarks, community skills and a v0.2 in six weeks is itself evidence that the shape meets a real need.

8 Experiments: without and with a knowledge bundle

The central empirical question of this guide, run live on 7 September 2026 on the same machine and fixture as the prior small-models benchmark: what changes when a small local model is given an OKF-style knowledge bundle?

8.1  Method

The knowledge under test is post-training-cutoff. All three models were trained before June 2026 and therefore have no parametric knowledge of OKF at all. Without a bundle, any “answer” about OKF is necessarily confabulation or an honest admission of ignorance; with a bundle, the facts are present in context. This isolates the effect of the knowledge artifact from the effect of the model.

  • Models: qwen3:1.7b (42.7 tok/s measured), qwen3:4b (19.5 tok/s at 8 K), gemma4:e4b (13.2 tok/s warm) — the Apache-2.0 tiers installed on this 8 GB Mac mini.
  • Fixture: identical to the machine’s prior benchmark (small-models.json): Ollama /api/generate, temperature 0.1, num_ctx 8192, one-shot turns, no conversation history. Output budget: 700 tokens — raised from an initial 200 after all three models consumed the whole budget inside their reasoning blocks and returned empty answers (think: false is passed but the installed builds ignore it). Three cells that still truncated or looped were re-run at 1,500–2,500-token budgets; every anomaly is logged in Appendix E and in kf-experiment/.
  • Bundle: a six-file, v0.2-shaped OKF bundle (~4 KB, ~1,090 tokens) covering OKF overview, v0.1 fields, v0.2 trust fields, bundle structure and attested computations, each concept a markdown file with YAML frontmatter, plus an index.md; reproduced in kf-experiment/bundle/.
  • Questions: five, each answerable entirely from the bundle: (1) what OKF is, who announced it and when; (2) the v0.1 frontmatter fields and which is required; (3) the v0.2 trust signals and the three tiers; (4) bundle structure and the roles of index.md/log.md; (5) attested computation fields and how the attester works.
  • Conditions: without (plain system prompt) vs with (same prompt plus the bundle wrapped in <knowledge> tags, with instructions to answer only from the bundle and reply “Not in bundle” otherwise). “Without” always ran first per model, so no answer could leak into the other condition.
  • Scoring: per-question rubric of required facts (e.g. Q2 requires type/title/description/resource/tags/ timestamp and that type is the only required field); each answer was machine-checked for rubric terms and read in full. Raw answers: kf-experiment/answers.md.

Prior work on this machine established the baseline the bundle now operates against: the 7 September benchmark (small-models.html) found the 1.7–3 B band reliable for extraction, classification and routing, the qwen3:4b context cliff (~1 tok/s at 32 K vs 19.5 tok/s at 8 K) decisive for context budgeting, and gemma4:e4b’s 9.6 GB bundle the reason to keep bundles small. It also recommended OKF bundles as the grounding fix for small-model fact errors (section A). This run is that recommendation, tested.

Reading of the results

Three findings stand out. First, hallucination on unknown facts is not a property of the model but of the missing context: every confabulation above is a model doing what language models do — producing the most plausible continuation — and the same models, given the facts, stopped confabulating entirely. Second, grounding dominates scale at the small-model tier: the 1.7 B model with the bundle outscored the 4 B and effective-4 B models without it, and matched them with it. Third, the residual errors are the interesting ones: honest “I don’t know” is rare without prompting for it, definitional slips survive grounding (runtime-as-duration), and nothing in this setup tests retrieval — the bundle was given whole, which is the favourable case. Selection, the layer Tenure found unsolved, is the next experiment (section E).

8.3  External comparisons and interpretation

Two published results bracket the local run. AWS’s sample OKF agent on BIRD mini_dev (the text-to-SQL benchmark) scored EX 74.0 across all 500 questions — above every published leaderboard entry under leaderboard conditions — with the agent reading only an OKF bundle over MCP: no CREATE TABLE statement was ever handed to it, and schema knowledge was reconstructed from the wiki alone at a mean of 4.47 reads per question, 96% of them structural (read_page + list_directory); semantic_search was available and never called. The authors are honest that the run differs from leaderboard rows in base model too, so it reads as “agent + OKF as a system clears the bar,” not as a model-controlled ablation.[AWS 2026]

Tenure’s benchmark is the counterweight: it tested what happens when the model itself decides which files to read across single turns and sessions. Alias resolution and ranking stability were clean wins for OKF’s file surface; scope disambiguation (4/12 passing), supersession exclusion (0/3), type routing, budget eviction and session drift were not — the bundle described the knowledge but did not govern retrieval. “Files are a format, not a retrieval policy.”[Tenure 2026]

Taken together with the local run, the picture: a bundle reliably grounds a small model (facts it cannot otherwise know), an index and file names make discovery work without vector search, and the unsolved layer is selection — what belongs in context for this turn. That layer is where the next round of experiments and products belongs (section E).

9 The knowledge life cycle

Knowledge is perishable. Every organisation runs a life cycle for it, whether or not it is managed: things are created, used, drift, get rediscovered or rot. The agentic era makes the cycle machine-visible, because each stage now has a field in a frontmatter block.

Who consumes has likewise widened. The consumers of a 2026 knowledge bundle are: humans searching, humans reading on a schedule, retrieval pipelines, agents deciding which files to read for a task, fine-tuning and evaluation pipelines, and audit systems checking what an agent was told and when. Each consumer class has different needs — an auditor needs the verification chain; an agent needs the file names and the staleness comparison; a human needs the title and description. A format that serves all four is the current design target.

9.2  The past: how knowledge died

The failure modes are well documented across four decades of knowledge management. Capture failure: expertise stayed tacit because externalising it cost time with no visible return (the “why should I write this down” problem Nonaka’s successors struggled with). Rot: documents that were accurate on creation day drifted as the world changed, with no mechanism to notice (Polanyi’s floor, again: the correction lived in people’s heads). Isolation: knowledge sat in tool-specific silos — a wiki here, a sharepoint there, a chat archive everywhere — each internally consistent, mutually incompatible, and unreachable at the moment of need. Version chaos: without a versioning discipline, the most recent copy and the most authoritative copy were different files. Enterprise KM systems of the 1990s–2000s tried to solve all of this by mandate and mostly failed, because they added a tax (filling in forms in a portal) to the people who held the knowledge and returned the benefit to people who did not. The wikis of the 2000s removed the tax but kept the rot: human maintenance is precisely what decays.

9.3  The present: docs-as-code, agents as maintainers

The present solutions share one move: treat knowledge like code. Git gives versioning, diffing, blame, review and rollback; docs-as-code puts runbooks and policies next to the systems they describe; markdown keeps the human cost of writing low. On top of that substrate, three new mechanisms close the old gaps:

  • Automated creation with citations. Enrichment agents draft concepts from sources of record and attach per-claim provenance, so creation scales and the human review has something concrete to check. The spec’s keyed-footnote design exists because agents rewrite documents; the attribution must survive rewriting.[OKF SPEC v0.2 §5.1]
  • Expiry as a first-class fact. stale_after turns “is this current?” into a deterministic comparison that any consumer can run, and makes re-verification schedulable rather than hopeful. Acme’s finance concepts carry stale_after: 2026-12-31 because policies are re-approved every January; on 1 January the concepts require re-verification before serving.[Google Cloud 2026b]
  • Agents as the maintenance workforce. Karpathy’s insight cuts both ways: the bookkeeping that decays human wikis — updating cross-references, touching fifteen files to propagate a change — is exactly what agents do reliably, given a verified source of truth and a review loop.

9.4  The future: knowledge operations as a discipline

Three trends shape the next phase. First, agentic curation: agents will propose the majority of changes and humans will increasingly review diffs rather than author documents; the scarce skill becomes verification, not drafting. Second, live knowledge: the attested-computation concept points to knowledge that is not a static statement but a sanctioned procedure with a per-run verdict — numbers that are true because they were computed the blessed way, not because someone wrote them down. Third, federated and market-shaped knowledge: bundles that cross organisational boundaries, with trust tiers standing in for the personal relationships that used to vouch for knowledge (section 11). The open question is the operating layer: who owns the retrieval policy, the re-verification cadence, the erasure obligations — a function that in 2026 does not yet have a standard name or a standard tool.

10 Discovery, communication, guardrails, efficiency

Four benefits of structured, standardised knowledge recur in the sources for this guide; a fifth class of benefits — audit, continuity, evaluation — is less discussed but equally real.

Discovery

Knowledge is only as good as its findability, for humans and agents alike. The OKF design attacks discovery twice: index.md directories give an agent a map before it reads anything (progressive disclosure), and per-file title/description/tags/type frontmatter gives it handles to route on. The evidence that this works is concrete: in the AWS BIRD run the typical agent did a couple of directory traversals then read 2–4 documents, 96% of calls were structural, and semantic_search — though available — was never called once across 500 agents.[AWS 2026] Tenure found the same mechanism from the failure side: alias queries (“GHA” → GitHub Actions, “Mongo” → MongoDB) resolved at 0.72 precision because filenames and titles are better handles than embedding similarity for short, code-shaped queries.[Tenure 2026] For deep multi-hop discovery, knowledge graphs remain the stronger technology (GraphRAG’s community summaries exist precisely because chunk-level retrieval misses global questions), which is why the pragmatic architecture is both: structure for navigation, embeddings for the long tail — the hybrid the machine’s own research recommended for small models.[Edge et al. 2024][prior work, section A]

Communication

Section 3’s boundary objects get a machine member. A knowledge bundle is how a team talks to its future self and to its agents at once: the same file that onboards a human (title, description, readable markdown) briefs an agent (frontmatter, links, trust fields). Across organisations, standardised knowledge removes re-negotiation: two companies exchanging bundles need not exchange schemas or parsers. Within a team, the bundle is the antidote to the tacit floor — the difference between an agent that can be onboarded by reading the index and one that needs a hallway conversation no agent can have.

Guardrails

Structured knowledge is the substrate of control. Four mechanisms matter:

  • Trust-tier filtering before reading. A consumer configured for human-reviewed content can refuse unverified or machine-confirmed concepts before spending a token on them — policy enforced at the metadata layer, not after the fact.[Google Cloud 2026b]
  • Expiry as a guard. stale_after makes “act on current policy” a checkable property; a stale concept can be surfaced, quarantined or refused rather than silently obeyed.
  • Attestation as an anti-improvising guard. An agent reporting a dollar figure must have run the sanctioned computation; the attester’s verdict gates display. This is the guardrail against the most dangerous agent failure: confident improvisation of numbers that look right.[OKF SPEC v0.2 §10]
  • Grounding for small models. On this machine’s L1 tier (1–4 B models), wrong specific facts are the primary failure mode, and the documented fix is a knowledge bundle: facts belong in retrievable, versioned documents, not in weights (section 8 tests exactly this).[prior work, section A]

Efficiency

The efficiency case is arithmetic. On this machine, qwen3:1.7b generates at 42.7 tok/s and processes prompts at ~900 tok/s; qwen3:4b collapses from 19.5 tok/s at 8 K context to ~1 tok/s at 32 K. Context is therefore the scarce resource, and bundles are a context technology: an index plus two concept files costs a few hundred tokens, not a retrieval dump. The AWS run quantifies the same economics for a capable agent: 4.47 reads per question on average, median 4, versus the alternative of shipping whole schemas or corpora into every prompt. Progressive disclosure is a latency and cost optimisation before it is anything else. Add the human side — one reviewed document consumed by many agents and many people, instead of each consumer re-deriving the knowledge — and the efficiency argument is complete.

The quieter benefits

  • Audit and compliance. “What was the agent told, by whom, verified by whom, until when” becomes answerable from frontmatter and logs — the record an EU AI Act or model-risk examiner asks for.
  • Continuity. Knowledge that survives staff turnover, tool changes and vendor exits is the difference between an organisation that learns and one that remembers.
  • Evaluation. Curated bundles double as golden sets: the same verified content that grounds an agent can score it, which closes the loop between knowledge management and model evaluation.
  • IP boundary clarity. Explicit provenance separates what the organisation owns, what it licenses and what is public — the precondition for the economics of section 11.

11 The economics of knowledge

Can knowledge be sold? Can it be protected as intellectual property? Can it be open source? The short answers: yes, imperfectly, and increasingly — and the agentic era is changing all three markets at once.

Selling knowledge

Knowledge is the canonical information good: expensive to produce, nearly free to copy, non-rivalrous in use.[Shapiro & Varian 1999] Markets for it are real and old — education, consulting, research and analytics, data licensing, expert networks, professional services, and now model fine-tunes and knowledge-as-a-service APIs. Information-good economics explains the observed pricing: sellers version (a summary is cheap, the verified bundle with audit trail is expensive), bundle (platform subscriptions), and sell scarcity of attention, verification or access rather than the facts themselves, because facts cannot be scarce. Two agentic-era wrinkles matter. First, the trust tiers of section 7.2 are a pricing signal: verified knowledge is the product, and the human-reviewed tier is where the margin lives. Second, agents are becoming buyers — an agent that pays per attested computation or per verified bundle is a new customer class with no patience for unlicensed scrapes. The open question is whether payment rails (micro-payments, receipts) will mature before the market fragments into walled catalogs.

Protecting knowledge as intellectual property

The law protects expression, not facts. The US Supreme Court’s Feist decision (1991) held that facts are not copyrightable and that “sweat of the brow” does not earn protection — a phone directory’s listings could be copied because originality, not effort, is the test.[Feist v. Rural 1991] The practical map for a knowledge owner:

  • Copyright protects the particular written expression of a concept (this article, a playbook’s wording), not the concept or the fact it states. Collections can be protected as compilations — the selection and arrangement — which is how curated datasets are defensible even when their facts are not.
  • Database rights (EU sui generis right) protect substantial investment in obtaining and verifying a database — closer to what a verified knowledge bundle actually is than copyright is.
  • Trade secrets protect knowledge that is secret, valuable and reasonably protected: algorithms, customer lists, pricing models, unreleased policy. This is the default regime for enterprise bundles behind access control — and it is why provenance fields matter: a bundle that cannot prove where its content came from cannot defend its secrecy either.
  • Patents protect inventions and, in some jurisdictions, methods — relevant to the processes of producing or attesting knowledge, not to the knowledge itself.
  • Licences (proprietary, Creative Commons, open-source) are the actual technology of knowledge transfer: they define what a buyer or recipient may do with expression, and they are the layer at which OKF’s permissive spec licence and a commercial “verified bundle” product can coexist.

Two structural ironies follow. A format designed to make knowledge portable also makes it exfiltratable: the same bundle that leaves a vendor cleanly can leave an employer. And the law’s fact/expression line means the most valuable layer of a knowledge product — the facts, the metrics, the definitions — is the least protectable, which pushes value toward the things the law does protect: verification, arrangement, timeliness and the human judgement in curation. The OKF authors’ decision to open the format while leaving content governance to producers fits this economics exactly.

Open-source knowledge

Knowledge can be open source, and the precedents are large: Wikipedia and Wikidata (CC BY-SA and CC0), open science and the FAIR data principles, open educational resources, and the open-weight model releases (Qwen3 and Gemma 4 are Apache 2.0; Llama carries a community licence with a scale clause; some small-model lineages remain research-only licences — a licensing lesson this machine’s prior research documented first-hand).[Wilkinson et al. 2016][prior work, section A] The economics of openness are classical: knowledge is a public good with large spillovers (Romer’s growth theory makes idea non-rivalry the engine of increasing returns), and commons governance can sustain shared resources without either the state or the market (Ostrom).[Romer 1990][Ostrom 1990] Openness also has business logic: an open standard is an ecosystem moat — Google open-sourced OKF not from altruism but because a portable format that hundreds of tools read increases demand for the clouds and catalogs that serve it best, the same playbook as Kubernetes, Chromium and MCP’s foundation transfer. The risks are real: free-riding, quality drift, and sustainability of the maintainers (Wikipedia runs on donations; open-source knowledge has no R&D tax credit). The emerging synthesis is hybrid: open format and open base knowledge, with revenue in verification, support, hosting and the human-reviewed tier — the same open-core pattern that dominates software.

The bottom line across the three questions: knowledge is the one asset whose value increases when it is shared and whose protection is weakest exactly where its value is highest. Formats, trust layers and licences are the mechanisms that reconcile the two — which is why this guide is about formats at all.

E Method notes and open questions

How the claims in this guide were produced, what they do not cover, and what to watch next.

Method notes

  • Article provenance. Written 7 September 2026 on an 8 GB Apple-silicon Mac mini. OKF content is grounded in the v0.2 SPEC and both Google Cloud announcements (shelf B1–B4); ecosystem and standards claims carry dated sources. Figures marked “as reported” are vendor- or press-reported and were not independently audited.
  • Experiment fixture. Ollama /api/generate, non-streaming; temperature 0.1; num_ctx 8192; think: false passed but not honoured by the installed builds — all three models reason anyway, so the output budget had to cover reasoning tokens; one-shot turns; conditions ordered without-then-with per model. Machine-readable: questions.json, results.jsonl, raw log answers.md, runner run_experiment.py, scorer score.py.
  • Run anomalies, disclosed. (1) The first full run used num_predict 200; every model spent the whole budget inside reasoning blocks and returned empty answers (eval_count == 200 with empty response). (2) The second run at 700 tokens produced 29/30 valid answers; qwen3:4b on “without” Q1 looped in reasoning mode across three attempts (700 tokens each, empty) and only answered after a 2,500-token rerun, and three other cells truncated mid-answer at exactly the cap (qwen3:4b “with” Q3, gemma4 “without” Q4/Q5), which were re-run at 1,500 tokens. Every rerun kept the original system prompt and temperature. The anomalies are themselves findings: on these small models, reasoning-mode token budgets must account for thinking overhead or answers silently vanish, and a 4 B model can loop indefinitely rather than admit ignorance on post-cutoff facts.
  • Scoring. Each answer was checked against a per-question rubric of required facts (field names, tiers, dates) both by string match and by full read; borderline cases were read twice. Rubrics and full answers are in the experiment folder for audit.
  • Limits. Five questions, one domain (OKF itself), one machine, three small models, one bundle given whole in the prompt. Whole-bundle injection removes retrieval error, which is the favourable case for OKF; it tests grounding, not selection. No vector-RAG baseline, no fine-tuned comparison, no frontier models, no multi-turn sessions. External results (shelf B5–B7) cover some of those gaps; none of them covers all.

Open questions

  1. The selection layer. Who decides which files enter context for this turn, with scope, supersession, recency and budget control? (Tenure’s “interchange, not selection.”) The strongest candidates: agentic retrieval (ACE line), graph engines (Graphiti, GraphRAG), and the memory-layer startups — none standardised yet.
  2. Discovery across bundles. index.md works inside a bundle; nothing yet indexes bundles across teams and organisations. A registry layer (llms.txt-style for bundles?) is missing.
  3. Vocabulary governance. OKF type values are unregistered by design; at scale, shared types (Metric, Playbook, Attested Computation) will drift across producers. Watch for community registries or SKOS-style bridges.
  4. Version negotiation. okf_version declares a target; consumers do best-effort. How bundles migrate across minor and major versions at fleet scale is untested.
  5. Memory interchange. The “memory has no standard” gap (section 5): session memory, long-term memory and knowledge bundles are three different things with one disputed name. The standard that separates them cleanly will shape the next product cycle.
  6. Evaluation standards. LongMemEval and PrecisionMemBench score memory and retrieval; there is no agreed benchmark for “agent grounded in a knowledge bundle” that varies bundle quality, trust tiers and staleness. The experiment in section 8 is a first step, not a standard.
  7. Erasure and law. The erasure-conformance profile answers deletion with proof; how it interacts with retrieval caches, embeddings and agent logs is unresolved and will meet the EU AI Act and GDPR head-on.
  8. Economics of verified knowledge. If human-reviewed bundles carry the margin (section 11), who certifies the reviewers, and does the trust tier become a market signal or a marketing label?
