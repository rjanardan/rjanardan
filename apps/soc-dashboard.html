Capability by era

How each stage of the work changes, in one table.

SOC 2016, pre-AISOC 2026, AI-era
DetectionSignature and correlation rules, static thresholds, IDS and antivirus feedsMachine-learning anomaly detection, UEBA, EDR telemetry, cross-source AI correlation
TriageEvery alert becomes a ticket; an analyst reads each one; 7 in 10 are noiseAI groups alerts into cases, scores risk, auto-closes noise; humans see the top 10-15%
InvestigationManual SIEM queries, log pivoting, copy-paste hash lookups, spreadsheetsAuto-built timelines, plain-language queries, sourced AI summaries, human verifies
ResponseEmail and phone calls to IT, change tickets, manual isolation via firewall GUISOAR playbooks with approval gates, containment in minutes, audit trail by default
ReportingHand-built spreadsheets and slides, weekly and monthly, always lateReports draft themselves from the case log; the analyst reviews, edits, and signs
StaffingTwo analysts per shift, on-call nights, high turnover, no time for huntingSame team size; hours move from triage to response, hunting, and model review
CoverageGaps found by accident; stale rules block nothing and nobody noticesAI surfaces stale detections, blind spots, and outdated agents before an attacker does
About these figures. Scenario model for a 2,400-person company, informed by public SOC benchmarks (SANS SOC survey, Verizon DBIR, vendor SOC telemetry) and the alert-fatigue literature. MTTA and MTTR are typical published ranges for small teams; treat them as orders of magnitude, not precise measurements. The queue simulation generates the numbers you see.

What stays the same across both eras is the operator: judgment, escalation timing, knowing the business, and the 02:00 phone call. AI changes which hours are spent on noise and which on decisions.

Built as a single self-contained HTML file. No frameworks, no telemetry, no external requests.
